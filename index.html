She had always been meticulous, arranging her life like a surgical tray—each instrument in its place, nothing superfluous. Her apartment, her schedule, her relationships: all curated with a pragmatist’s disdain for the ephemeral. So when the city’s ubiquitous scaffolding began to appear, first on her block, then on every corner, she saw it not as an eyesore but as a promise of order restored.

But the renovation dragged on. The dust seeped through her sealed windows, a fine gray film that exacerbated her allergies and settled on her white sofa. The precarious wooden walkways, which she navigated with careful, clipped steps, became a daily gauntlet. One morning, a worker’s radio blared a cheerful pop song, a sound so jarringly human it made her flinch. She looked up, past the netting, and saw a man in a hard hat, laughing with his colleague, his lunchbox open beside him. He wasn’t thinking about the final blueprint. He was thinking about the warmth of the sun.

That evening, she found a small, yellow wildflower growing from a crack in the concrete, right where the scaffold’s shadow fell. It was superfluous to the design, a mistake. She knelt, her clean trousers brushing the grime, and for the first time, she didn’t want to fix it. She just let it be.
