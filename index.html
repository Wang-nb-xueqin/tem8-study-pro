At the café on the corner of Bleecker and Sullivan, the rain was doing its usual superfluous work, soaking the awning seams and the shoulders of men who had forgotten umbrellas. I watched a woman across the room—mid-thirties, pragmatic in her beige trench coat—methodically arrange three sugar packets into a perfect triangle. Her meticulousness was almost aggressive, a small rebellion against the day's chaos.

Then her phone buzzed. She read the message, and her face did something complicated: a tightening around the eyes that suggested the news was not good, and that she had expected it anyway. She did not cry. Instead, she crumpled the sugar packets—a gesture that felt both ephemeral and definitive—and left a five-dollar bill under the saucer. The precarious balance of her composure held, barely. Outside, the traffic’s honking seemed to exacerbate the silence she left behind.

I thought about how our grief is so often ubiquitous yet invisible, carried in subway cars and office lobbies like a low-grade fever. The barista wiped the table with a damp cloth, erasing the triangle's ghost. She had already moved on, as we all must, to the next mundane task. The rain kept falling, indifferent. Her story, like most, would never be told—only performed, briefly, in the careful folding of a napkin and the precise click of a heel on wet pavement.
